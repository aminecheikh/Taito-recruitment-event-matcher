## 1. Key takeaways

1. **Structure beats freestyle.** Structured, job-related interviews predict job performance better than unstructured ones (about .44 vs .33 operational validity; McDaniel, Whetzel, Schmidt & Maurer, 1994). Building the event around the recruiter's top 4 requirements is the best-supported part of our direction.
2. **Coaching can raise applicant performance without ruining validity.** Maurer and colleagues found that content-focused interview coaching helps; the more comprehensive the coaching, the better the ratings, partly because it raises applicants' knowledge of what to do (Tross & Maurer, 2008; Maurer, Solamon & Lippstreu, 2008). Coaching applicants is therefore defensible, not cheating.
3. **Concrete past-behaviour evidence is the core technique.** Behavioural event interviewing, critical incident technique and STAR all ask for specific past episodes (situation, own action, result). This is the right target for the applicant's "real examples" *(unverified here; standard method, see section 2.1)*.
4. **Authenticity helps strong candidates.** Applicants with a stronger drive to be seen as they really are got more offers when they were already rated highly, and an expert rater judged them less inauthentic (Moore et al., 2017). The benefit did not hold for low-rated candidates, so it is not a magic fix. This supports "AI asks, never writes".
5. **Self-efficacy is the lever for job-search interventions.** The JOBS programme (Vinokur and colleagues), built on Bandura's theory, improved re-employment and mental health, mostly for high-risk participants. Evidence is mixed on whether the self-efficacy change itself drives job finding.
6. **AI coaching evidence is mixed.** Early trials showed a chatbot coach matching human coaches on self-reported goal attainment (students, Terblanche et al., 2022). A later head-to-head trial with senior leaders reportedly found no AI effect over control while human coaching worked (de Haan, Terblanche & Nowack, 2026; only secondary summary seen). Design for AI as preparation support, with humans at the event.
7. **Strengths inventories have modest, mostly observational support.** VIA-based interventions show small-to-medium effects on wellbeing-type outcomes (Hedges' g = 0.32 in a 2022 meta-analysis). None of this is evidence of predicting hiring fit, so use them as prompts, not as scores.
8. **Employer side is under-researched in what we found.** Job analysis and critical incidents for roles are established practice *(unverified here)*, but we found no peer-reviewed work on forced-choice prioritisation of requirements. Treat our top-4 ranking as a design hypothesis to test in the hackathon, not an evidence-backed method.

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 Eliciting concrete evidence

- **Behavioural event interviewing (McClelland) and critical incident technique (Flanagan)**: ask for specific real episodes, what the person thought, did and the outcome, rather than opinions or traits. McClelland's 1973 paper "Testing for competence rather than intelligence" (American Psychologist) is the usual origin *(unverified; not opened)*. Flanagan 1954, "The critical incident technique", Psychological Bulletin *(unverified)*.
- **STAR/CAR (Situation-Task-Action-Result / Challenge-Action-Result)**: a practitioner format for answering such questions. We found no peer-reviewed evaluation of STAR itself *(unverified)*. The structured-interview validity evidence above is the nearest support.
- **Cognitive interviewing (Fisher & Geiselman)**: context reinstatement and "tell me more detail" prompts improve recall *(unverified; originates in forensic interviewing)*. Useful for helping people who say "I can't think of an example".
- **Structured interview validity**: McDaniel, Whetzel, Schmidt & Maurer (1994), "The validity of employment interviews: a comprehensive review and meta-analysis", Journal of Applied Psychology. PDF seen in search: https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/645/articles/McDanieletal1994CriterionValidityInterviewsMeta.pdf

### 2.2 Questioning styles

Motivational interviewing (Miller & Rollnick; OARS = open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries), Socratic questioning, appreciative inquiry (Cooperrider), solution-focused questions (de Shazer; scaling, exceptions, "miracle question") and laddering (from means-end chain research; keep asking "why does that matter?") are all established question styles *(unverified here; not searched)*. Common principle for us: the coach elicits and reflects, the person supplies content. That matches the AI Act constraint that AI must not author the applicant's answers.

### 2.3 Career counselling theory

- **Career construction / life-design (Savickas)**: a narrative approach where clients tell stories of early memories, role models and turning points, and re-author them toward the next step. It suits career changers and international talent whose CV does not show their story. Evidence is mostly small and uncontrolled: a 2026 replication with 19 participants reported gains in vocational certainty and identity and less distress; case studies reported greater self-awareness. We found no pooled effect size. Sources: https://papers.iafor.org/submission83395 (intervention, intensity, outcomes); https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1381233/epub (2024 mini-review noting lack of standardised tools).
- **Self-efficacy (Bandura) and job-search interventions**: JOBS II randomised field experiment, 1,801 recent job losers, found earlier re-employment at better-paying jobs and lower depressive symptoms, with effects concentrated in high-risk participants (less education, women, higher psychological risk). A follow-up reported self-efficacy rose at one and six months but the rise was not itself tied to finding work; the absolute level was. Sources: https://mprc.isr.umich.edu/projects/jobs/ ; https://mprc.isr.umich.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Vinokur1997.pdf . An independent youth job-search study found no lasting self-efficacy or employment differences (https://www.blind.msstate.edu/sites/www.blind.msstate.edu/files/2021-06/Long-Term-Effects-Job-Search-Youth.pdf), so effects are not guaranteed.
- **Strengths approaches**: A 2022 meta-analysis of 29 articles found a small-to-medium effect (Hedges' g = 0.32) of strengths-based positive psychology interventions, and the authors say data are too limited to replace symptom-focused approaches. A 2018 meta-analysis of "use your signature strengths" reported gains in happiness and life satisfaction but not negative emotions. A 2025 meta-analysis (130 studies, N = 275,007) found correlations of roughly .10 to .30 between most character strengths and healthy functioning, which is observational. Sources via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4406142 and https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/08902070251366765 (titles and exact authors not confirmed; *(unverified)* for citation purposes). VIA's own site summarises its research and has a commercial interest. **CliftonStrengths**: not searched; we have no verified evidence either way. Do not rely on it.

### 2.4 Interview anxiety and preparation

- Tross & Maurer (2008), experiment with 144 participants and three coaching levels: the most comprehensive coaching produced the highest ratings, with interviewee knowledge as a partial mediator. Maurer (2007, Personnel Psychology) describes a structured-interview coaching programme designed to raise performance without harming validity. Maurer, Solamon & Lippstreu (2008, Journal of Organizational Behavior, DOI 10.1002/job.512 as listed in search) compared content-focused coaching with score-gaming tactics. Details beyond abstracts not verified. Index: https://zendy.io/title/10.1348/096317907x248653 ; https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/job.512
- McCarthy & Goffin (2004), "Measuring job interview anxiety: Beyond weak knees and sweaty palms", Personnel Psychology, introduced a multi-dimensional interview anxiety measure. We could not open it; details *(unverified)*. Interview anxiety is commonly linked to lower interview ratings, with the likely mechanism being behaviour and narrative quality, not ability *(unverified)*.

### 2.5 Helping employers clarify needs

Job analysis, critical incidents for roles ("describe a time someone in this role excelled or failed"), "first 90 days" questions, and separating must-have from trainable are standard practice in selection work *(unverified here)*. We found no peer-reviewed source on forced-choice ranking of requirements. Likely pitfalls from general judgement research *(unverified)*: stated importance diverges from real choices, people inflate "must-haves" under social pressure, and forced ranking hides ties. Mitigation: ask about observable outcomes, not credentials; ask what they would train; allow "this is a tie" and capture a reason.

### 2.6 Authenticity and rehearsal

Moore, Lee, Kim & Cable (2017), "The advantage of being oneself: The role of applicant self-verification in organizational hiring decisions", Journal of Applied Psychology 102(11), 1493-1513, DOI 10.1037/apl0000223 (fetched via https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/800). Two field samples plus a mock-interview study with text analysis: among candidates the organisation rated in the top 10%, stronger self-verifiers were more likely to get offers; raters saw them as less inauthentic. The effect depended on candidate quality. No source found directly comparing rehearsed vs spontaneous answers *(unverified)*. Safe design position: rehearse the content (examples), not the script.

### 2.7 Human vs AI coaching

- Terblanche et al. (2022), PLOS ONE: two equivalent 10-month trials; human and AI coaches both beat controls and AI matched human coaches on self-reported goal attainment; undergraduate students; self-report. https://doaj.org/article/404b88a4fa3b4d5799600dcd0fb8371b
- Terblanche et al. (2022), International Journal of Evidence Based Coaching and Mentoring: replication RCT of the Vici chatbot (75 treatment, 94 control); goal attainment rose significantly, other measures were non-significant. https://doaj.org/article/a22056d18a03481baf70910354cd3223
- de Haan, Terblanche & Nowack (2026), Human Resource Development International: three-arm RCT, 114 senior leaders; per secondary summary, AI coaching showed no significant improvement over control, human coaching did. We saw only the intro and commentary, so *(unverified)*.

## 3. Question bank

**For applicants** (AI asks, applicant answers in own words)

1. Think of a recent time you did [requirement #1] for real. Where were you and what was needed? *(BEI / STAR: situation)*
2. What exactly did you do yourself, as opposed to the team? *(BEI: own action)*
3. What happened as a result, and how do you know? *(STAR: result)*
4. If you cannot think of a work example, where else have you done this: studies, volunteering, family, a hobby, in another country? *(Career construction, broadened sources)*
5. Take yourself back to that day. What was the first thing you noticed? Who else was there? *(Cognitive interviewing: context reinstatement)*
6. What was the hardest moment in that story, and what did you try first? *(Critical incident)*
7. What were you thinking just before you decided to act? *(BEI: thoughts and intentions)*
8. What did you learn that you do differently now? *(Career construction, re-authoring)*
9. On a scale of 0 to 10, how well does this example show [requirement]? What would make it one point higher? *(Solution-focused scaling)*
10. Tell me about a time this went better than you expected. What was different that time? *(Solution-focused exceptions / appreciative inquiry)*
11. What did your colleagues or friends thank you for most? *(Appreciative inquiry)*
12. Why does that matter to you? And why does that matter? *(Laddering)*
13. So what I hear is that you [reflection of their words]. Did I get that right? *(MI reflective listening; AI must not rewrite)*
14. Which of these companies' top 4 do you feel you have the strongest example for? Which do you have none for? *(Self-efficacy: mastery-based, honest gap spotting)*
15. If you had one minute at the table, which of your examples would you start with and why? *(Self-selection and preparation, MI evoking)*
16. Which of your examples would a recruiter in your new country not recognise without explanation? *(Career construction, international context)*

**For recruiters**

17. Describe the best person who ever held this role. What did they do in their first 90 days that you noticed? *(Critical incident, job analysis)*
18. Which of these requirements would you teach on the job, and which must the person arrive with? *(Must-have vs trainable)*
19. If you could only check four things in a 10-minute chat, which four would they be? *(Forced-choice prioritisation)*
20. What is the reason a hire in this role failed in the past? *(Critical incident, negative)*
21. For your number one requirement, what would a good answer sound like, and what would a weak one sound like? *(Behaviourally anchored criteria)*
22. Where two requirements feel equal, what tips the balance? *(Surfaces ties and reasoning)*
23. What do you list on the posting that you would drop if you had to? *(Pruning the long list)*
24. What does an applicant need to show you that a CV cannot? *(Evidence of the core need)*

## 4. So what for our design

- **Interview shape**: build the event conversation around the top 4 and use STAR-style evidence prompts. Provide the same 4 prompts to all applicants to preserve structured-interview fairness.
- **AI role**: ask, probe, reflect, and flag gaps; never draft, polish or suggest wording. Show applicants the probe types, not content. This fits the rules and the authenticity evidence (Moore et al., 2017).
- **Probe ladder** for each example: situation, own action, result, one cognitive-interviewing detail prompt, one scaling prompt. Stop after about 5 turns to avoid fatigue.
- **No example available**: offer broadened sources (volunteering, studies, home country) before declaring a gap. Let the applicant say "no example" and still decide to attend; self-selection stays theirs.
- **Confidence building**: show the applicant a checklist of "examples ready: 3 of 4" (mastery experience), not a score or ranking of the person. Avoid any person-level scoring, which would risk profiling under the AI Act.
- **Recruiter side**: constrain ranking to 4, require a one-line observable behaviour per item, allow "tie", and ask for a "teachable" tag. Test the ranking pitfalls with the Espoo team.
- **Keep humans in the room**: the evidence for AI-only coaching is thin and mixed. Use AI before the event only; humans run the conversation and any assessment.
- **Strengths inventories**: optional prompt material, never an input to matching.
- **Evaluate**: measure applicant-reported confidence and recruiter-reported usefulness of the conversation; do not claim performance gains we have not measured.
